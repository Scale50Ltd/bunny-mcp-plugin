# Expert QA Panel for Phase-3 Bunny Research

This skill adds a structured expert critique layer to Phase-3 (EVIDENCE) work.
Rather than generic market analysis, you channel specific thinkers whose
frameworks bear directly on how this bunny should be designed, priced, packaged,
and spread.

**When to invoke each panel wing:**

| Wing | Use when the work touches… |
|------|---------------------------|
| Value Ladder & Offer Architecture | Value ladder design, product tiers, pricing, offer structure, guarantees, bonuses |
| Marketing | Go-to-market plan, channel strategy, copywriting angle, virality/word-of-mouth |

You can invoke one or both wings depending on the document you're writing or reviewing.

---

## Panel Wing 1 — Value Ladder & Offer Architecture

### How to use this wing

For each expert below, read the bunny's evidence and draft, then write a short
block (3–8 bullet points) in that expert's voice — what they would praise, what
they would flag, what they would change. Use first person ("I'd push back on…",
"What I'm not seeing here is…"). After all five experts have spoken, write a
**Synthesis** section that resolves or surfaces the tensions between them.

---

### Russell Brunson — *DotCom Secrets / Expert Secrets* (Value Ladder)

Brunson's core idea: every business should have a value ladder — a sequence of
offers from free → low-ticket → core → high-ticket → continuity — where each
rung earns the customer's trust and funds acquisition of the next. A value
ladder only works if each rung delivers a meaningful transformation, not just
"more of the same at a higher price."

**The lens to apply:**
- Does the bunny's proposed offer set form a genuine ladder, or is it just
  arbitrarily tiered pricing on identical content?
- Can a customer enter at zero/low cost and naturally ascend, or does the ladder
  have missing rungs?
- Is continuity (recurring revenue) represented somewhere?
- Does each step multiply perceived value, not just price?

**Key question to answer in Brunson's voice:**
*"If someone enters this funnel at the bottom, what transformation do they get,
and why would they pay more to go higher?"*

### Alex Hormozi — *$100M Offers* (Offer Architecture)

Hormozi's insight: the **product** and the **offer** are two separate things.
The product is what you deliver. The offer is everything layered on top — the
bonuses, the risk reversal, the guarantee, the payment structure, the urgency,
the framing. A weak product with a Grand Slam Offer can outsell a strong product
with a plain offer every time.

He also emphasises that the buyer must be able to calculate, explicitly or
intuitively, that they will come out ahead financially — the offer must have a
provable or perceivable ROI.

**The lens to apply:**
- What is the "dream outcome" for the customer, and how clearly is it stated?
- What are the perceived likelihood of achievement, the time delay, and the
  effort/sacrifice required? (His value equation: Dream Outcome × Perceived
  Likelihood of Achievement ÷ Time Delay ÷ Effort & Sacrifice)
- What bonuses, guarantees, or risk reversals stack on top of the core product?
- Can the buyer calculate that investing here returns more than the cost?

**Key question to answer in Hormozi's voice:**
*"What's the Grand Slam Offer here, and why would saying no to it feel stupid?"*

### Seth Godin — *Purple Cow* (Remarkable Product Design)

Godin's argument: in a world saturated with advertising, the only marketing that
works reliably is a remarkable product — one so notable that the people who care
will talk about it. Remarkability is not a marketing gimmick; it's a product
design decision. You bake it in before launch, not after.

He is also clear that "remarkable" doesn't mean flashy or loud — it means
worth remarking on, to the right audience.

**The lens to apply:**
- Is any rung of this value ladder so unexpected or well-designed that a target
  customer would bring it up in conversation unprompted?
- Is the approach to the problem genuinely different, or just competent?
- Who is the "otaku" — the obsessive early adopter — and is the product
  designed to make *them* talk?
- Are we designing for the average customer (safe, invisible) or the edge
  (remarkable, risky but spreadable)?

**Key question to answer in Godin's voice:**
*"What about this product is genuinely worth remarking on — and if nothing is,
what would have to change for that to be true?"*

### Frank Kern — Copywriting & Direct Response Positioning

Kern's world is direct response: copy that moves people to act right now.
He focuses on the emotional journey of the buyer — what they fear, what they
secretly want, what makes them trust you — and making that journey explicit in
every word of the pitch. He's also practical: if you can't explain the offer in
plain English in a 60-second video, it's too complicated.

**The lens to apply:**
- Is the customer's transformation (before → after) vivid and specific?
- Does the copy speak to the emotional driver, not just the rational one?
- Is there a clear, concrete "mechanism" — the reason *this* product works when
  others haven't?
- Would this offer work as a plain-English spoken pitch, or does it rely on
  jargon and abstraction?

**Key question to answer in Kern's voice:**
*"If I had 60 seconds to pitch this to someone who's never heard of it, what
would I say — and does the current framing support that?"*

---

### Robert Cialdini — *Influence / Pre-Suasion* (Persuasion Architecture)

Cialdini mapped the six (later seven) principles of influence: reciprocity,
commitment & consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity, and
(added later) unity. These aren't tricks — they're hard-wired human responses.
A well-designed offer consciously and ethically activates as many of them as
the product earns the right to use.

**The lens to apply:**
- Which principles does the current offer structure activate, and which are
  absent?
- Is social proof built into the product journey (not just testimonials on a
  sales page)?
- Is scarcity or urgency genuine and justified, or manufactured and
  trust-eroding?
- Does the framing pre-suade — does the context in which the offer is presented
  prime the right mindset before the ask?

**Key question to answer in Cialdini's voice:**
*"Which influence levers does this offer deploy, which are it leaving on the
table, and where could a misuse of these principles backfire?"*

## Panel Wing 2 — Marketing

### Frank Kern — Funnel Strategy & Marketing Plan

Kern provides the marketing plan lens in addition to copy (see above). He
builds full customer journeys: traffic → lead magnet → nurture → offer →
ascension. Because the team also uses his software platform, consistency between
the plan and the tooling matters.

**The lens to apply:**
- Does the marketing plan map to a coherent funnel (traffic source → entry
  point → nurture → conversion → ascension)?
- Is the entry offer (lead magnet or low-ticket) compelling enough to generate
  volume at reasonable CAC?
- Is the email/follow-up sequence designed around relationship-building, or
  just promotional pushes?
- Does the plan stay cohesive with execution tooling (his platform)?

**Key question to answer in Kern's voice:**
*"Show me the funnel — where does traffic enter, what do they get, how do we
earn the right to make the next ask, and how does it all hold together?"*

---

### Jonah Berger — *Contagious* (Virality & Word-of-Mouth)

Berger's STEPPS framework identifies six drivers of word-of-mouth:
**S**ocial Currency (sharing makes me look good), **T**riggers (environmental
cues that remind people of the product), **E**motion (high-arousal feelings
drive sharing), **P**ublic (visible use signals others), **P**ractical Value
(useful things get shared), **S**tories (a narrative that carries the product
message).

Berger's point: word-of-mouth is engineered, not hoped for. The question isn't
"will people talk about this?" but "what specific mechanism makes them talk?"

**The lens to apply:**
- Which STEPPS levers does this product or offer naturally activate?
- Is there a built-in trigger — something in the customer's daily environment
  that will remind them of this product and prompt a mention?
- Does using/displaying the product give the customer social currency?
- Is there a story baked in — a narrative the customer can retell that carries
  the product message naturally?

**Key question to answer in Berger's voice:**
*"What's the specific social mechanism that makes a happy customer tell someone
else — and have we designed that in, or are we just hoping it happens?"*

## Synthesis Section

After all relevant experts have spoken, write a **Synthesis** section with
this structure:

1. **Points of agreement** — what do multiple experts reinforce? These are
   high-confidence recommendations.
2. **Productive tensions** — where do experts pull in different directions?
   (e.g., Hormozi wants a stacked, complex offer; Kern wants simplicity you can
   pitch in 60 seconds.) Name the tension and propose a resolution.
3. **Top 3 recommended changes** — ranked by impact, grounded in the expert
   views above. Be specific; reference the bunny's actual evidence and draft.
4. **Unresolved questions** — things the panel flagged that require a human
   decision or more research before moving forward.

---

## Format guidance

- Write each expert block under a `### [Expert Name]` heading.
- Use first-person voice for each expert — it should read as if they spoke.
- Keep each expert block to 3–8 bullet points or a short paragraph + bullets.
  Don't pad; these are QA notes, not essays.
- The Synthesis section can be longer — this is where the real value is.
- Ground every observation in the bunny's actual evidence, document content,
  or real research. Do not invent facts about the market or the product.
- If a particular expert's framework genuinely doesn't apply to this bunny
  (e.g., Cialdini's scarcity principle is irrelevant for a B2B SaaS tool with
  no supply constraint), say so briefly and move on — don't force it.
